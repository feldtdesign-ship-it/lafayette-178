The space

A white box with a black steel face

One clean room, 18 feet 2 inches wide and 25 feet deep, under a white pressed-tin ceiling with ten bare bulbs in two rows. Pale grey tile underfoot, white plaster walls, a split system over the door. Behind it, a bath and the stair down to a dedicated 13 by 41 foot cellar for stock.

The storefront is one run of heavy black steel: a display window, a pair of glass doors, a second display window, with louvred vents above and a plain black fascia ready for one name. Ownership delivers white box with HVAC.

SoHo meets Nolita

Lafayette is the seam between the cast-iron district and the little streets east of it. Foot traffic comes both ways: the SoHo shopper walking east, the Nolita local walking west.

On the 6

Spring Street and Canal Street on the 6 are each a four-minute walk. Canal Street's N, Q, R, W, J and Z are five. Prince Street's R and W are seven.

A landmarked front

The building is a 1906 walk-up inside the SoHo-Cast Iron Historic District Extension. The red fire escape and cream brick above the shop are the backdrop.

The store

Prints on one wall, tees on the other, the figure in the window

Straight down the room from the door.

The gallery wall

The south wall is hung with PJ's prints in one level row of black frames: the line drawings of New York characters that started the whole thing on the subway platforms.

The shelving wall

The north wall is matte black pipe and white oak plank, planks of folded tees above and a rail of hangers below, one colourway to a bay: cream, navy, lilac, moss, peach.

The floor

Two white oak tables carry the folded stock and the current drop. A black counter sits by the back door. A Galaga cabinet stands in the back corner, because every PJ store has one.

The window

Gumbit, the bubblegum-pink suit-and-tie character, stands four feet tall on a black plinth in the north window, lit from above, facing Lafayette Street. The south window carries the signature in white and the newest work.

The business

Fourteen years of selling directly to New York

On September 26, 2012, PJ O'Rourke II wheeled a hand-built art cart onto an MTA subway platform and started selling his prints to whoever walked past. No connections, no capital, no permission.

Born in Tulsa, he dropped out of college, landed on a friend's couch in Astoria with one month to figure it out, and built the cart from a single Home Depot trip, metal clips and chicken wire. The subway became his first gallery: raw, public and unforgiving. For eight years he sold directly to the public underground, refining the voice and building a following the hardest way possible.

In 2014 the pop-ups started in DUMBO, and the prints became apparel on a used heat press. In 2019 he opened his first storefront in the East Village, at 11th Street and First Avenue. The shop, the online store and the Instagram have run ever since.

Why 178 Lafayette

Small, bright, and on the right street

A 700 square foot flagship on a corridor that people already walk is the format PJ has proved for a decade: tight, curated, built for content and conversation, with the stock downstairs and the window doing the work.

The room needs almost nothing done to it. The tin ceiling, the tile and the white walls stay. The fit-out is freestanding: pipe-and-plank shelving, oak tables, frames on the wall. In and trading fast, out cleanly.

The window is the sign. A four-foot pink figure facing Lafayette Street, lit at night, with the signature in white on the glass beside it.
